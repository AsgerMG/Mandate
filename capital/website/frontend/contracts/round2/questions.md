## Purpose

Screen 1 scopes. Round 2 does two jobs, and may not compromise on either.

**Job 1: narrow the funnel to the funds that actually publish.** This is where
a wide candidate pool becomes the handful the founder sees. A question earns
its place by changing which funds come out on top, not by resolving a gate on
a fund nobody will read about.

**Job 2: understand the commercial model.** Distinct from job 1 and not
subordinate to it. These answers need not touch eligibility at all; they exist
so the brief can position the company correctly, and they are the only source
for anything the brief says about who the company sells to.

## One screen, planned as a whole

The screen is planned in a single pass that sees every candidate question at
once, plus everything the founder has already told us. It is not three
generators whose output is deduplicated afterwards.

  **evidence:** three generators previously ran in parallel and could not see
  each other, and none of them received the founder's screen-1 answers. A
  screen asked for technology readiness twice in two framings, the founder
  answered differently to each, and the brief published both numbers about one
  product. Four separate downstream dedupe rules had been added over time and
  none of them caught it.

**Four questions is the target. Six is the ceiling, not the budget.** The cap is
enforced in code; the target is a judgement the planner makes. A screen that
asks four sharp questions is better than one that fills six, and questions
exist to change which funds publish or to make positioning writable — not to
demonstrate thoroughness.

## Two halves, two disciplines

The **gate half** is written from a fund's own requirement text and asks
nothing a real rule does not support. That discipline is right for
eligibility, because an invented requirement is a lie about the law.

The **commercial half** is deliberately not gate-grounded. No fund encodes
"who is your customer" as an eligibility rule, so under the gate discipline
the brief could never learn it, and positioning was being written from a
sector label.

  **evidence:** a company selling a subscription product directly to
  individuals was advised to position on institutional deployment and outcomes
  for a buyer it does not sell to.

The commercial answers are firewalled: they may shape positioning and the
payoff bullet, and the eligibility verdict never reads them. A wrong answer
must be able to weaken prose and never to move a legal conclusion.

## Rules

- Q-1 (hard) Answer options are written in the founder's own commercial
  vocabulary, translated out of the requirement rather than quoted from it. An
  option carrying a verbatim run of words from the fund's text has been
  lifted, not translated.

  **evidence:** an option read as a phrase from a state-aid regime document
  rather than anything a founder would say about their business. It was
  selected, and it reached the brief as a claim about what their financing
  covered.

- Q-2 (hard) No two questions ask the same thing in different words. Funds
  phrase shared requirements differently, so the same question can arrive
  twice from two instruments. Two questions measuring on one scale (readiness
  levels, percentage bands, headcount) are one question however framed. This
  is a backstop: the planner sees every candidate at once and is the thing
  that should prevent it.
- Q-11 (judged) Never ask what the founder has already answered. The known
  facts are supplied with the candidate pool; a question they settle is a
  question already asked.
- Q-3 (hard) Every question offers at least two substantive, mutually
  exclusive options. Nothing that restates the question, and no catch-all: the
  interface appends its own "none of these".
- Q-4 (hard) At most one question per gate. A founder is never asked twice for
  one rule.
- Q-5 (hard) The commercial half runs whenever the founder has described their
  company, regardless of whether any eligibility gate is still open.
- Q-10 (hard) A question and the options beneath it must answer the same
  thing. A question whose answer is yes or no may not be published over a list
  of choices, and a question asking which of several things applies may not be
  answered only yes or no.

  **evidence:** a multi-select of commitments a founder might make was
  rendered under a yes/no question about a public investor taking a board
  seat. Neither half was wrong alone, which is why nothing caught it.

- Q-6 (judged) A founder can answer from memory, without opening a document.
  If answering would require having read a work programme, a call text or a
  regulation, the question does not belong on the screen whatever gate it came
  from.
- Q-12 (judged) Ask in the founder's world, never the programme's. The
  requirement is WHY a question matters; it never supplies the words.

  The test, applied per word rather than against a list: **could this founder
  have used this word themselves, about their own business, before they had
  ever heard of this fund?** If a word exists only because a programme
  document uses it, it does not belong in the question or its options. This
  holds for vocabulary from funds we have never encountered, in any language,
  which a list of banned phrases would not.

  Where a technical scale genuinely is the clearest way to ask, describe the
  rungs in plain words and put the term in brackets, never the reverse.

  **evidence:** a screen opened with whether the company's work was "aligned to
  a specific Horizon Europe call topic" and asked "which eligible cost category
  under GBER" the funding targeted. Both are answerable by a grants consultant
  and by nobody else, and they make an intelligence product read as paperwork.

- Q-13 (judged) Do not ask what the founder's own description already answers.
  Where the description is close but genuinely ambiguous, quote their words in
  the question and ask for the specific distinction that is still open. A
  cold question about something they just told us reads as not having read it.
- Q-7 (judged) Questions name what this company actually does, in the
  founder's own words, so the screen reads as written for them.
- Q-8 (judged) Ask about the project and the business, never about legal form,
  headcount or country: screen 1 settled those.
- Q-9 (soft) Fewer, sharper questions beat more. A question whose answer would
  not change which funds publish, or how the company is positioned, is a
  question not worth asking.

## Banned

- Q-B1 Inventing a requirement the fund's text does not support.
- Q-B2 Asking the founder to interpret statute language on the product's
  behalf.
