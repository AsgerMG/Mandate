What has to be true, step by step — and what we are doing about it

Each step has one condition it must satisfy for the founder to get the right answer. Where it does not, the work in flight is named, with what it unlocks.

  1. 1

    The founder tells us about themselves

    holds

    Must be true: we learn enough in one screen to narrow 1,100 funds to a workable shortlist. It does. No work needed here, and we have measured that adding questions at this stage buys nothing.

  2. 2

    We build the shortlist

    —% holds

    Must be true: the fund they would actually have won is in the shortlist. It is, almost every time. This is the asset — everything downstream is about not squandering it.

    This number has not moved and is not the problem. The shortlist is about 105 funds and it contains the answer. What happens to it next is the problem, and it is the step below — which nobody ever wrote down.

  3. !

    Then we cut the list from 105 funds to 18

    37% the whole loss is here

    This is not one of the six steps. It is a trim that sits between building the shortlist and showing it to the AI, and it is where 98% becomes 37%. Two founders in three are handed a list that no longer contains the fund they would have won — so nothing downstream, however good, can help them. Every other number on this page is a rounding error next to this one.

    Why eighteen? Being established now. The likeliest answer is a cost limit from when asking the AI was expensive, never revisited — but this project's most reliable source of wasted effort is a number whose original reason quietly expired, so it is being checked rather than assumed. The one thing already ruled out is simply raising it: that was tried and made the answer worse, for reasons nobody has explained.

  4. 3

    The AI asks what it still needs to know

    fixed today

    Must be true: the questions come from looking at these funds. This step is live today, for every founder, and the questions really are written against the funds in front of them — quoting each fund's own requirement, word for word.

    The list was cut short, and now is not. Roughly three in ten of the founder's own funds were dropped before the AI saw them, because their reference codes were longer than a limit nobody had revisited. One test founder kept none of their six. Worse, the questions still named the right funds while the requirements underneath described a shorter set — so it read correct. Across twelve test founders, 43 of 72 of their own funds got through before; all 72 do now.

  5. 4

    The founder answers

    holds, after today

    Must be true: what they type reaches the thing that chooses, and cannot delete a fund by accident.

    Half of it was being thrown away. The questions about the business itself — who pays you, what an award would buy — were discarded the moment the founder submitted them, on every request, because they were not attached to a particular fund. Nobody had noticed: the part of the system that writes the memo still kept a section reserved for answers that could never arrive. Fixed today. The other half now holds and is guarded: across twelve test founders and 672 answers, not one answer removed a fund.

  6. 5

    The AI picks the five

    —% built and verified — merges when the test finishes

    Must be true: it can tell the funds apart. Blank cards are effectively gone — rows saying nothing at all fell from 22% to 4%, and Estonia, the Netherlands, Italy and Denmark are now at zero. And as of today the system can act on 3,697 written rules across 1,018 funds, up by about 480. Until this morning those rules were prose: they changed the wording of a memo and decided nothing.

    The decision was taken to ship the AI ranker regardless of the test, because the arithmetic it replaces does not scale. It is built and verified; it merges the moment the paid test finishes, because that test measures the ranker against the very code the change deletes. Nothing is deployed yet and this line will say so until it is.

    What a founder will see, measured on twelve test companies: nine of twelve get a different set of funds. 13% of the funds published are new, and every one of them carries an obstacle — seven with a single named hurdle, four not yet established. Eleven funds with no obstacle at all drop out to make room. That is the trade: the old arithmetic promoted no obstacles above right fund. No fund with a shut door is published, and each new row states its hurdle in the fund's own words.

  7. 6

    Five funds and their next steps go into the memo

    —% reads well, but it is also deciding

    Must be true: the founder can act on it. It reads well, and we have deliberately not spent effort on the writing — that is not where the accuracy is lost.

    But the writer is quietly making the final cut. Any fund it declines to write about is dropped from the founder's five without a word. Nobody chose it for that job; it is a side effect of how the memo is assembled. It survives the change above, so even once the AI ranks the funds, the last word still belongs to whichever one the writer felt it had something to say about. Named here because it is the next thing to fix, not because it is fixed.

How we get to 90% — the real arithmetic

Every figure on this page used to come from a different test on a different set of founders. Four numbers, four exams — and comparing them produced two confident conclusions that were simply wrong. There is now one measurement, run on one group of 897 companies whose funding we already know, following the right answer the whole way through.

  still there lost here
The corpus holds the fund they won 100%
… and we are allowed to offer it at all 91% −9
… and this founder's own answers keep it 88% −3
… and its written rules do not rule them out 68% −20
… and it survives the cut to eighteen 39% −28
… and it survives the writer's cap of eight 28% −11
… and it is one of the five they read 20.5% −8

The honest number is 20.5%. Not the 98% we quote for finding funds, and not the 51% the AI scores — those measure different things on different people, and both are true of what they measure.

Two losses dominate, and neither is the AI. Twenty points go to funds ruling the founder out on their own written rules, and twenty-eight to the cut to eighteen. Together that is nearly half of everything, and it happens before the AI is asked anything.

The cut is now understood, and it is cheap to undo. It was never a cost decision — it was a stopgap for three problems that have all since moved elsewhere. Widening it to forty would put the right fund on the list for 58% of founders instead of 37%, for about one extra euro-cent per founder. The old worry that a longer list makes the AI worse is contradicted by our own test: on funds sitting deep in the list, the AI found them 22 times out of 51 where the old arithmetic found them none.
